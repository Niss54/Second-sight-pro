WHO Guidelines on Antibiotic Stewardship and Rational Use of Antimicrobials
Source: WHO Global Action Plan on Antimicrobial Resistance (2015) + WHO AWaRe Classification (2021)

SECTION 1 — WHO AWaRe ANTIBIOTIC CLASSIFICATION
Access antibiotics (first-line, widely available):
- Amoxicillin, Amoxicillin-Clavulanate, Ampicillin
- Cefalexin (Cephalexin)
- Doxycycline, Trimethoprim-Sulfamethoxazole (Co-trimoxazole)
- Metronidazole, Nitrofurantoin

Watch antibiotics (second-line, use only when first-line fail):
- Ciprofloxacin, Levofloxacin (Fluoroquinolones)
- Azithromycin, Clarithromycin (Macrolides)
- Cefuroxime, Ceftriaxone (Cephalosporins 2nd/3rd gen)
- Piperacillin-Tazobactam, Meropenem (for hospitalised patients only)

Reserve antibiotics (last resort, hospital only):
- Colistin, Vancomycin, Linezolid, Carbapenems
- Must NEVER be used empirically in community settings.

SECTION 2 — COMMON INFECTIONS — FIRST-LINE TREATMENT (India Context)
Community-Acquired Pneumonia (CAP) — Outpatient mild:
- Amoxicillin 500mg three times daily × 5 days (adults without comorbidities)
- Amoxicillin-Clavulanate 625mg twice daily × 5 days (if comorbidities)
- Azithromycin 500mg daily × 5 days (if atypical pathogen suspected)
- DO NOT use fluoroquinolones as first-line for CAP in outpatients.

Urinary Tract Infection (UTI) — Uncomplicated:
- Nitrofurantoin 100mg MR twice daily × 5 days (first choice)
- Co-trimoxazole 960mg twice daily × 3 days (if local resistance <20%)
- Avoid Ciprofloxacin for uncomplicated UTI to preserve its effectiveness.

Acute Bacterial Skin Infections:
- Cefalexin 500mg four times daily × 5–7 days
- Cloxacillin/Flucloxacillin for Staphylococcal infections
- Add Metronidazole only if anaerobic infection suspected (bite wounds, perianal).

Acute Otitis Media (children):
- Amoxicillin 40–45 mg/kg/day in 2–3 divided doses × 5–7 days
- Watch and wait (analgesia only) appropriate for mild cases in children >2 years.

SECTION 3 — ANTIBIOTIC PRESCRIBING RED FLAGS
DO NOT prescribe antibiotics for:
- Common cold, rhinitis, sore throat (unless Group A Strep confirmed)
- Acute bronchitis in otherwise healthy adults
- Most cases of acute diarrhoea (self-limiting; antibiotics worsen antibiotic-associated diarrhoea)
- Influenza

ALWAYS check:
- Allergy history before prescribing any antibiotic
- Renal function before prescribing Nitrofurantoin (contraindicated in eGFR <30), aminoglycosides, or high-dose beta-lactams
- Drug interactions before prescribing macrolides, fluoroquinolones, or metronidazole

SECTION 4 — CONFLICTING ANTIBIOTIC PRESCRIPTIONS — WHAT TO DO
When two doctors prescribe different antibiotics for the same infection:
1. Identify the suspected organism and site of infection first.
2. Use WHO AWaRe classification to determine which antibiotic is more appropriate.
3. Prefer the Access-category antibiotic unless clinical features suggest resistance or severe infection.
4. NEVER combine two antibiotics from the same class (e.g., two fluoroquinolones, or two macrolides) — no clinical benefit, additive side effects.
5. If unsure, a culture and sensitivity result should guide therapy.

SECTION 5 — ANTIMICROBIAL RESISTANCE IN INDIA
- India has the highest burden of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) globally.
- E. coli resistance to fluoroquinolones in India exceeds 70% in community-acquired UTIs in many regions.
- MRSA prevalence in India: ~40% of all S. aureus infections.
- Carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella (CRKP) and E. coli are emerging; require specialist input.
- This means: empirical fluoroquinolone treatment for UTI often fails in India; culture before or alongside treatment is strongly recommended.

Safety Disclaimer:
Antibiotic choice must be guided by local resistance patterns, clinical severity, and patient factors.
